# Module 1 — Planning as Design

*Read time: 15 minutes. Exercise: 30 minutes.*

> **The idea in one line:** A plan isn't a list of what you'll do. It's a decision about what kind of life you're building.

---

## The idea

Most people meet planning as administration. A diary, a task app, a list on the back of an envelope. Its job is to stop things falling through the cracks.

That's a useful job, but a small one. Planning done that way keeps you organised inside whatever life you already have. It never asks whether that's the life you want.

There is a second kind of planning, closer to what a designer or an architect does. You start from the experience you want to create — how a guest feels walking into a hotel lobby, how a regular feels when their order is ready before they reach the counter — and work backwards to the details that will produce it. The details matter enormously, but they serve the experience. They don't replace it.

Applied to a life, this means starting every planning session with a different question. Not *"What do I need to do?"* but *"What do I want to be true?"*

The shift sounds minor. It isn't. A task list made from the first question is a pile of obligations. A plan made from the second is a set of choices — and choices carry energy that obligations never do.

### Busy is not the same as moving

You've almost certainly had a week where everything got done and nothing happened. The inbox was cleared, the meetings were attended, the reports went out. And on Friday, the thing that would actually change your business, your health or a relationship was exactly where it was on Monday.

That week wasn't a failure of effort or discipline. It was a failure of design. Nothing in the system asked which of those activities led anywhere.

### The three questions

The whole method rests on three questions. Module 3 covers them in depth. For now, just get familiar with them:

1. **Result** — What do I actually want? (The outcome, not the activity.)
2. **Reason** — Why does it matter to me? (The fuel.)
3. **Route** — What's the simplest path there? (The action plan, kept short.)

Notice the order. Most people start with the third question and never get to the first two. They know *what to do*. They've lost track of *what for*.

---

## The method: build your Life Map

Before you can plan a week well, you need to know what the week is in service of. That's the job of the Life Map.

A Life Map divides your life into five to seven **areas**. Each area gets:

- **A name** that means something to you
- **A vision**: one or two sentences describing what "excellent" looks like in that area, written in the present tense
- **A current standard**: an honest description of where it is today
- **A role**: who you are when you're at your best in that area

### Step 0 — Write the long list first

Before you organise anything, let yourself want things. Set a timer for twelve minutes and write, without stopping, everything you'd like to be, do, have, learn, experience or give at some point in your life. Big and small. Practical and slightly absurd. Keep the pen moving and don't edit.

Robbins uses a version of this exercise in *Awaken the Giant Within*, splitting the list into four headings — personal growth, career and business, experiences and possessions, and contribution — with a few fast minutes for each. Then, for each item, write how many years you'd give it: one, three, five, ten or twenty. Finally, circle the four or five one-year items that excite you most, and write a short paragraph on why you're absolutely committed to each.

The point isn't the list itself. It's to remind yourself what you want before deciding what you'll do. You'll find your areas, visions and first Results hiding in it.

### Step 1 — Name your areas

Common areas include health, relationships, family, finances, work, business, growth, home, contribution. Use whichever fit, but name them in your own words. Names that sound like you hold your attention far better than names that sound like a form.

Some examples of names people have chosen:

| Generic | Personal |
|---|---|
| Health | *Strength & Stamina* |
| Business | *The Coffee House* |
| Career | *Commercial Leadership* |
| Relationships | *The People Who Matter* |
| Finances | *Freedom Fund* |
| Personal growth | *The Craft* |

Five to seven areas. Fewer and you'll leave something important unattended. More and you won't be able to hold them in your head.

### Step 2 — Write a vision for each

Write it as if it's already true. Be specific enough that you'd recognise it on sight.

- Weak: *"Be healthier."*
- Better: *"I train four mornings a week, sleep seven hours, and have the energy for a full day of client work followed by a proper dinner at home."*

The vision doesn't need to be realistic this quarter. It needs to be true to what you want.

### Step 3 — Describe the current standard

One or two honest sentences. Not harsh, not flattering. *"I train when the week allows, which is about once. I'm tired by 4pm most days."*

The gap between the vision and the standard is where your plans will come from.

#### Shoulds and musts

There's a useful distinction here from Robbins. Most of what we'd like to improve lives in the category of *should*: I should train more, I should call the advisors, I should look at the margins. Shoulds rarely happen. What changes a life is when something moves from *should* to *must* — when you decide that the current standard is no longer acceptable to you.

You won't do that for every area at once, and you shouldn't try. But as you write each current standard, ask: *"Is this still acceptable to me?"* The areas where the honest answer is *no* are where you'll find your energy for the next quarter.

### Step 4 — Name the role

Who are you in this area when you're at your best? This sounds soft. It's one of the most practical tools in the course.

*Commercial Leadership* might carry the role **The Trusted Advisor**. *The Coffee House* might carry **The Host** or **The Architect**. When you're unsure how to handle something in that area, the role tells you. A trusted advisor doesn't send a pushy follow-up. A host doesn't let the queue go unacknowledged.

## Exercise 1.0 — The long list

Do Step 0 before anything else: twelve uncensored minutes, timeframes beside each item, then circle your top four or five for the coming year and write a paragraph of reasons for each. Keep this list. You'll return to it at every Quarterly Review.

## Exercise 1.2 — The gap

For each area, give yourself a score from 1–10 for how close the current standard is to the vision. Then answer:

1. Which area, if it improved by two points, would lift every other area with it?
2. Which area have you been quietly neglecting because another one is louder?

Your answer to the first question is your **lead area** for this course. Most of your exercises in Modules 3–5 will draw from it.

## This week's practice

Each morning, before you open email or messages, read your Life Map. It takes ninety seconds. You're not trying to act on it yet — just to start each day reminded of what the day is for.

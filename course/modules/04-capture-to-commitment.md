# Module 4 — From Capture to Commitment

*Read time: 20 minutes. Exercise: 60 minutes (your first weekly session).*

> **The idea in one line:** Once a week, empty your head, group everything by the result it serves, and commit to the essential few. This hour saves you many more.

---

## The idea

The three questions work for a single goal. A real week has dozens of demands competing for the same hours. You need a way to bring them all together, make sense of them, and decide.

That's the **Weekly Mise en Place**: a 60-minute session, once a week, at the same time. It is the engine of this method. If you only keep one habit from this course, keep this one.

It has five stages:

1. **Capture** — get everything out of your head
2. **Group** — cluster items by the result they serve
3. **Plan** — write a Result, Reason and Route for each group
4. **Commit** — choose your musts and put them in the calendar
5. **Prepare** — set up the first day

---

## Stage 1 — Capture (10 minutes)

Write down everything that's on your mind. Everything. Work and personal, large and small, urgent and someday.

- Calls to make, emails to send, decisions to take
- Projects in motion and ideas not yet started
- Things you've promised people
- Things you're worried about
- Things you keep meaning to do

Go through your sources as well: inbox, messages, notes app, last week's plan, your calendar for the next fortnight, anything written on scraps of paper.

**The rule:** don't organise yet. Don't judge. Just get it all onto one page. The aim is an empty head.

Most people capture between 40 and 100 items the first time. That number is the weight you've been carrying around all week.

---

## Stage 2 — Group (10 minutes)

### First, clear what doesn't belong

Before grouping, run through the list once and make a quick decision on each item. Laura Stack's **6-D system**, built for handling incoming information, works well here:

| Decision | What it means here |
|---|---|
| **Discard** | It doesn't need doing. Cross it out. |
| **Delegate** | It needs doing, but not by you. Mark who. |
| **Do** | It takes under two minutes. Do it after the session, in one batch. |
| **Date** | It needs you, but not this week. Give it a date and move it to your Later list. |
| **Drawer** | It's information, not an action. File it where you'll find it. |
| **Deter** | It keeps arriving and shouldn't. Unsubscribe, change the process, or add it to your Not-To-Do list so it stops coming at all. |

Most people find that a third of their capture list disappears at this step. What's left is what you actually need to plan.

### Then group by result

Now look for items that belong together because they serve the same outcome. This is the step most planning systems miss, and it's where the overwhelm starts to lift.

A list of 60 items is exhausting. Six groups of ten is a plan.

Group by **result**, not by type. Not "all my calls" and "all my emails," but "everything to do with the Q1 advisor programme" and "everything to do with the winter menu." A call, an email and a decision can all live in the same group.

Some items won't fit a group. That's fine. Put them in one of two places:

- **Maintenance** — routine things that keep life running (pay the rent, renew the insurance, restock cups). These don't need a Reason; they need a time slot. Batch them into one or two blocks a week.
- **Later** — ideas and someday items that don't belong in this week. Keep a separate list so you know they're safe. (Stack calls this the Master list: the running record of everything you want to do eventually, kept apart from what you'll do today so it can't crowd the day.)

Label each group with the Life Map area it belongs to.

---

## Stage 3 — Plan (20 minutes)

For each group, write a short plan using the three questions:

- **Result** — what will be true at the end of this week?
- **Reason** — two or three lines. Why it matters.
- **Route** — the actions in this group, with the musts marked.

This doesn't need to be as thorough as the plans in Module 3. A few lines per group is enough. The point is that every action now sits inside a result with a reason attached.

You'll often find that writing the Result makes half the actions unnecessary. Cross them out.

---

## Stage 4 — Commit (15 minutes)

Now be ruthless. You can't do everything you've captured this week, and pretending you can is how weeks go wrong.

1. **Choose your weekly Results.** Usually between three and six. One per area is a good default for areas that need attention this week. Some areas can rest.
2. **Mark the musts.** For each Result, the two or three actions that would produce most of it.
3. **Total the time** your musts require. Compare it to the time you really have, after meetings and commitments.
4. **Cut until it fits.** If the musts add up to 30 hours and you have 18 free, something has to go. Decide now, rather than on Thursday. Often this means going back to someone and saying no, or not yet — see *Saying no* below.
5. **Block the time.** Put every must in the calendar as an appointment with yourself. Group similar actions in the same block where possible.

### Must time

Give your musts your best hours. For most people that's the morning, before the day's demands arrive. Protect at least one block of 90 minutes or more each day for must actions only — no messages, no meetings, no inbox.

This is the single most effective scheduling habit in the course. A few protected hours a week, spent on the right work, will outperform a week of scattered effort. Weaver puts it well: it's a daily appointment with the person most important to your success — you.

### Estimate honestly, then tighten

Work expands to fill the time you give it (Parkinson's law). Weaver suggests an experiment: once you've estimated how long a must will take, try scheduling it for half that time and see what happens. Some tasks will fit; others won't, and you'll learn where your estimates are generous. Don't apply this to work that needs real depth — a strategy, a menu, a difficult letter. Use it on the tasks that tend to sprawl.

### Saying no

You can't protect must time without declining things. Stack is direct about this: most of us are too generous with our time, and people will take what we offer. Some principles adapted from her chapter on saying no:

- **Decline warmly, and clearly.** *"Thank you for thinking of me — I can't take this on right now."* No long explanation is owed.
- **Don't promise what you won't deliver.** A clear no lets the other person find someone else. A vague maybe leaves them stranded.
- **If you need time, say so — then answer promptly.**
- **When you can't refuse, negotiate.** *"I'm working on X, Y and Z. If this comes first, which of those should move?"*
- **Offer a smaller yes.** An introduction, a short call, an advisory role instead of a full commitment.

In hospitality and luxury sales, saying no well is a skill clients notice. A clear, courteous no builds more trust than a yes you can't honour.

## Stage 5 — Prepare (5 minutes)

Look at tomorrow (or Monday). Write your first [Daily Card](../tools/daily-card.md):

- Three Results for the day, each linked to a weekly Result
- The musts for each, in the order you'll do them
- The time of your protected block

Close the session. You're done.

---

## The daily version

Each morning, take five to ten minutes:

1. Look at your weekly plan.
2. Check what changed since yesterday.
3. Write today's Daily Card: three Results, their musts, your protected block.

Don't open the inbox until the card is written. Weaver cites the efficiency rule of thumb that every minute of planning saves several in execution. The exact ratio is debatable; the direction isn't. If you don't plan your day, you spend it helping other people with theirs.

### Triage when the day is too full

Some days, even your three Results won't fit. Stack borrows a tool from emergency medicine: triage. Rather than first come, first served, sort by severity. A simple version for the Daily Card:

| Level | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **P1** | Serious consequences if it isn't done today | Proposal due to a client at 17:00 |
| **P2** | High-value, not urgent — becomes a crisis if neglected | The Q1 advisor plan |
| **P3** | Someone will be disappointed eventually | A supplier wants a call back |
| **P4** | Doesn't matter; often a comfortable escape | Tidying the inbox, scrolling |

Do P1s first. Protect at least one P2 every day — this is where your future is built, and it's the first thing to vanish when you don't plan. Batch the P3s. Drop the P4s. And decide in the morning which items you'd let go if the day turns, so you're not deciding under pressure.

Each evening, take two minutes:

1. Tick what got done.
2. Move anything unfinished to tomorrow or back to the weekly plan.
3. Note one thing that went well.

---

## When the week falls apart

It will. A client crisis, a sick team member, a delayed shipment. The plan doesn't fail when this happens; it becomes more useful. Because you know your weekly Results and their musts, you can rebuild in five minutes:

1. What's the new reality?
2. Which Results still matter most this week?
3. What's the one must I can still protect today?

Then do that one thing.

## Exercise 4.1 — Your first Weekly Mise en Place

Choose a time this week — ideally Sunday evening or Friday afternoon — and do the full session using the [Weekly Mise en Place template](../tools/weekly-mise-en-place.md). Time each stage.

Afterwards, answer:

1. How many items did you capture?
2. How many groups did they form?
3. How many hours did your musts add up to, before and after cutting?
4. How did you feel at the start of the session, and at the end?

## Exercise 4.2 — Fix your weekly appointment

Put a recurring 60-minute block in your calendar for the Weekly Mise en Place. Give it a name you'll respect. Treat it like a meeting with your most important client.

## This week's practice

Do the Daily Card every morning. Protect one block of must time every working day, even if it's only 60 minutes. On Friday, count how many of your weekly Results you achieved.

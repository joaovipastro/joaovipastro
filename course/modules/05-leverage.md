# Module 5 — Leverage

*Read time: 20 minutes. Exercise: 45 minutes.*

> **The idea in one line:** Your time should go where only you can add value. Everything else should be stopped, handed over, or turned into a system.

---

## The idea

There's a stubborn belief, especially among owners and senior people, that doing more yourself is a sign of commitment. That staying late, answering every message and checking every detail is what good leadership looks like.

It's usually the opposite. Every hour you spend on work someone else could do is an hour not spent on the work only you can do — the relationships, the decisions, the standards, the direction. The business doesn't grow past the limit of your personal capacity, and your life gets smaller to fit around the business.

Leverage means getting more result from less of your own effort. It isn't about working less for its own sake. It's about putting your best hours where they change the most.

### Why people resist it

- **"It's faster if I do it."** True the first time. False by the fifth.
- **"Nobody does it as well as I do."** Possibly. But do they do it *well enough*? And could they, with a better brief?
- **"I don't have time to train someone."** Which is why you'll still be doing it next year.
- **"I like doing it."** The most honest reason, and sometimes a good one. Keep a few things for yourself on purpose. Just know which ones and why.

## Start with the 80/20 map

Before deciding what to change, find where your results actually come from. Weaver's suggestion is to look at your business through the 80/20 lens, in two directions:

1. **Which few clients, products or activities produce most of your revenue or results?**
2. **Which ones take most of your time?**

Put the two lists side by side. The interesting cases are the mismatches: the high-value client you rarely speak to, the menu item that sells little but takes the most prep, the account that absorbs your week and returns very little.

| | Produces a lot | Produces little |
|---|---|---|
| **Takes a lot of my time** | Protect and systemise | Simplify, renegotiate, or stop |
| **Takes little of my time** | Give it more attention — this is where growth hides | Leave it alone |

For a luxury travel business, this often reveals that a handful of advisors or agencies drive most bookings, and deserve far more personal attention than they get. For a coffee shop, it often shows that a few drinks and a few hours of the day carry the margin.

## The five levers

For any recurring piece of work, there are five options other than doing it yourself forever.

### 1. Stop

Some work doesn't need doing at all. It exists because it was done last year, or because someone once asked for it. Ask: *"What would happen if this simply stopped?"* If the answer is "nothing much," stop.

Examples: a weekly report nobody reads closely. A meeting whose decisions could be made by message. A social media channel that brings in no customers.

### 2. Simplify

Some work is necessary but bigger than it needs to be. Ask: *"What's the 20% of this that produces 80% of the value?"*

Examples: a 15-page proposal that could be five pages and more persuasive. A month-end close with steps that check the same thing twice. A menu with 30 items where 12 sell.

### 3. Systemise

Some work happens again and again in roughly the same way. Write it down once, properly, and it stops needing you.

- Checklists (opening and closing, client onboarding, event setup)
- Templates (proposals, follow-up emails, supplier orders)
- Standards (*"this is what a perfect flat white looks like, and how to check"*)
- Automations (invoice reminders, booking confirmations, CRM follow-up tasks)

A good system lets someone else produce your standard of work without you in the room. In hospitality, this is the difference between a great shift when the owner is present and a great shift every day.

### 4. Hand over

Some work needs a person, but not you. Delegate it — properly.

A good handover has five parts:

1. **The Result** — what does "done well" look like?
2. **The Reason** — why it matters. People do better work when they understand the purpose.
3. **The boundaries** — budget, deadline, what they can decide alone and what needs you.
4. **The resources** — templates, contacts, examples of good work.
5. **The check-in** — when you'll review, and how.

Notice that the first two are the same questions you ask yourself. You're not handing over a task. You're handing over a Result, with its Reason attached. That's what lets people use judgement instead of waiting for instructions.

### 5. Borrow

Some work is slow because you're working it out from scratch. Robbins calls learning from people who've already succeeded the most powerful way to compress time: trial and error costs years; a good model costs a conversation or a book. Before starting anything new, ask: *"Who has already done this well, and what can I learn from them?"*

Examples: a fellow owner who's already opened a second location. A commercial director who's built an advisor programme in another market. A roaster whose wholesale model you admire. One well-prepared lunch can save you a season.

### Close the loop

Stack makes a point worth keeping: leverage isn't a one-off clear-out. Systems drift, bottlenecks move and new work creeps in. Once a quarter, look again at where work gets stuck, waiting on you or on someone else, and fix the bottleneck rather than working around it.

## Find your only-you work

Before you can decide what to hand over, you need to be clear about what to keep. Your **only-you work** is the small set of activities where your involvement makes a disproportionate difference.

It usually falls into a few categories:

- **Relationships** that depend on you personally — key clients, partners, investors
- **Decisions** that set direction or carry real risk
- **Standards** — defining what excellent looks like, and holding people to it
- **Creative or strategic work** that draws on your particular experience and taste
- **Developing people** who will in turn take work off you

For a commercial director in luxury travel, this might be: top-tier partner relationships, pricing and positioning decisions, the annual commercial strategy, coaching the sales team. Not: building the deck from scratch, updating the CRM, chasing contract signatures.

For a coffee shop owner: sourcing relationships, the menu and the guest experience standard, hiring and culture, the financial picture. Not: the weekly supplier order, the rota, posting on Instagram.

## Exercise 5.1 — The time audit

Look at your calendar and your Reactivity Audit notes from Module 2. List everything you spent time on in a typical recent week. Aim for 20–40 items.

For each, estimate hours per week, then mark it:

| Activity | Hrs/wk | Only me? | Lever |
|---|---|---|---|
| | | Y / N | Keep / Stop / Simplify / Systemise / Hand over / Borrow |

Total the hours in each column.

## Exercise 5.2 — Your Leverage Ledger

Using the [Leverage Ledger template](../tools/leverage-ledger.md), choose **five** items from the audit where a lever would free the most time or energy. For each, write:

- The lever you'll use
- The first step, and when you'll take it
- Who's involved
- The hours per week it will free once done

Then add the freed hours together. That's the time you're about to reclaim. Decide now — in writing — what you'll do with it. If you don't, it will fill back up with reaction.

## This week's practice

Every time you start a task, ask: *"Is this only-me work?"* If it isn't, add it to the Ledger, even if you do it anyway this time. By Friday you'll have a clear picture of where your leverage is hiding.
